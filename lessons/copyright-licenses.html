Who owns it?

Every song, photo, video, and line of code you find online belongs to someone. In this lesson you’ll learn what copyright protects, when you can use someone else’s work anyway, and how creators can choose to share.

Copyright basics

Anything you create, whether a song, a drawing, an essay, or a program, is your intellectual property. Copyright is the legal right to decide whether, and how, your work can be copied.

You don’t have to apply for copyright or add a © symbol. In the United States and the 180-plus countries that follow the international Berne Convention, a work is copyrighted the moment it’s fixed in a tangible medium: written down, saved, or recorded. Creators can register a copyright, which helps if they ever need to enforce it in court, but it isn’t required.

Protected

The way an idea is expressed: your words, your drawing, your code.

Not protected

The idea itself, and plain facts. Anyone can write their own story about a wizard school.

In the U.S., copyright gives the creator these exclusive rights:

  • Copy (reproduce) the work
  • Make derivative works based on it, like a remix, translation, or sequel
  • Distribute copies by selling or renting them
  • Display or perform the work in public, including streaming it

Decide whether copyright protects each one.

Copyright doesn’t last forever

In the U.S., copyright on most works made since 1978 lasts for the creator’s life plus 70 years. After that, the work enters the public domain, where anyone can use and adapt it without permission. Enter the year a creator died to find out when their work becomes free to use.

Older works follow different rules. U.S. works published before 1978 are protected for 95 years after publication, which is why the 1928 cartoon Steamboat Willie, Mickey Mouse’s debut, entered the public domain in 2024.

Key idea

Your work is your intellectual property, and it’s copyrighted automatically as soon as it’s fixed in a tangible form. Copyright protects how ideas are expressed, not the ideas themselves, and it eventually expires so works enter the public domain. Presenting someone else’s work as your own is plagiarism.

2

Fair use

You run into other people’s copyrighted work all the time: a movie review that shows a short clip, a news story that quotes a song, a class handout with a paragraph from a book. In the U.S., uses like these can be legal under fair use, which allows limited use of copyrighted material for purposes like criticism, commentary, news reporting, teaching, and research.

There’s no simple rule. Only a judge can decide whether a use is fair, by weighing four factors:

1. Purpose

Educational or commercial? Does it transform the work into something new, like commentary or parody, or just copy it?

2. Nature of the work

Using factual work is more likely to be fair than using highly creative work like fiction or music.

3. Amount

How much was used, and was it the “heart” of the work?

4. Market effect

Does the use replace the original, so people don’t need to buy it?

Weigh the factors

A YouTuber posts a 10-minute review of a new movie. The review uses four short clips, each about 5 seconds long, and the channel earns money from ads.

Likely fair, or likely not?

For each situation, decide which way a judge would probably lean.

Key idea

Fair use allows limited use of copyrighted work for purposes like commentary, news, teaching, and parody. A judge weighs the purpose, the nature of the work, how much was used, and the effect on the original’s market. Using a small part in a new, transformative way is most likely to be fair.

3

Copyright in the digital age

When copying a book meant owning a printing press, few people could break copyright law. Now anyone can copy and share a song, video, or image in seconds, often without realizing they’re violating copyright.

The rise and fall of Napster

1999

Napster launches, a free app for sharing MP3 files between users’ computers. Millions use it to download copyrighted music without paying.

2000

The band Metallica, the rapper Dr. Dre, and major record labels sue Napster for enabling copyright violations.

2001

A court orders Napster to block copyrighted songs. It can’t block enough of them, and it shuts down.

Today

Legal services like Spotify, Apple Music, and Amazon pay royalties to copyright owners each time a song is streamed or bought.

Digital rights management (DRM)

Once you’ve paid for a song, what stops you from sending it to a thousand friends? Many copyright owners use digital rights management (DRM): technology that restricts how and where you can use digital media. For example, songs you download in a streaming app are scrambled so they only play inside that app, and many e-books only open in one company’s reader.

The DMCA

Programmers can often find ways to break DRM. In 1998, the U.S. passed the Digital Millennium Copyright Act (DMCA), which does two main things:

  • It makes it illegal to make or share tools that get around DRM.
  • It lets copyright owners send takedown notices to websites hosting their work without permission. The site must remove the work or risk legal action.

Copyright owners have asked Google to remove billions of links from its search results this way, mostly links to pirated content.

Concerns about DRM and the DMCA

  • Mistaken and abusive takedowns. One 2016 study found that roughly a third of the takedown requests it examined were questionable: many weren’t about copyright at all, and others targeted likely fair uses. Some people have used takedowns to silence critics or competitors.
  • Accessibility. DRM can stop screen readers from reading an e-book aloud, which cuts off people who are blind or visually impaired. Getting around the DRM could break the DMCA, so advocates push for exemptions for accessibility tools.
Key idea

The ease of copying digital files raises legal and ethical questions. DRM restricts how digital media can be used, and the DMCA makes it illegal to get around DRM and lets owners demand takedowns. Both protect creators, but they can also block fair uses and people who rely on assistive technology.

Creative Commons

What if you want people to share and build on your work? You can attach a license: a legal statement that gives others permission to use your work, under conditions you choose. You keep the copyright; the license just loosens the rules.

Creative Commons (CC) is a non-profit that offers six free licenses for creative works like writing, photos, music, and video. Every CC license requires attribution (BY): anyone who uses the work must credit the creator. The licenses differ in three optional conditions:

NC: NonCommercial

The work can’t be used to make money.

ND: NoDerivatives

The work can be shared, but not changed.

SA: ShareAlike

If you change it, you must share your version under the same license.

Build a license

You took a great photo and want people to use it. Answer two questions to find your Creative Commons license.

Can people change it?

There’s also CC0 (“CC zero”), which gives up all rights and puts a work in the public domain. More on that in section 6.

Decide whether each use is allowed by the work’s license.

Give credit the right way

A good attribution answers four questions, which Creative Commons sums up as TASL: Title, Author, Source (a link to the original), and License. Try it with a made-up photo.

Your attribution

You’ll find a work’s license near the work itself: in an image caption, a video description, or a website’s footer. Many search tools, like image search, let you filter results by license. Wikipedia, for example, shares its articles under CC BY-SA.

Key idea

Creative Commons licenses let creators keep their copyright while giving others permission to use their work. Every CC license requires attribution; some also limit commercial use (NC), changes (ND), or require remixes to use the same license (SA). Using CC work means following all of its conditions.

5

Open source

Most apps have a lot in common: buttons, forms, logins, databases. If every programmer wrote all of that from scratch, there would be far fewer apps. Instead, programmers build on open source code: code that’s published online with a license letting anyone use, study, change, and share it.

An open source project keeps its license in a file named LICENSE in its main folder. Two common kinds:

Permissive, like the MIT License

Use the code for anything, even in a product you sell, as long as you keep the original copyright and license notice.

Copyleft, like the GNU GPL

If you share software built from the code, you must release your version under the same license, like ShareAlike.

This page is an example. It makes your PDF using jsPDF, an open source library shared under the MIT License. Writing a PDF maker from scratch would take weeks.

Open code can also be risky. Because anyone can read it, attackers can study it for weaknesses, or even sneak harmful code into a project. In 2018, an attacker took over a popular JavaScript library and added code that tried to steal from cryptocurrency wallets.

Key idea

Open source licenses let programmers reuse, change, and share code. Permissive licenses like MIT ask only for credit; copyleft licenses like the GPL require shared versions to stay open. Open source saves time and spreads the work across many contributors, but popular code can also be a target for attackers.

6

Public domain and open access

The public domain

A work in the public domain has no copyright restrictions at all. Anyone can use it, change it, or sell it, and they don’t even have to give credit. Works get there in two ways:

  • Their copyright expires. The plays of Shakespeare, the music of Mozart, and the paintings of Rembrandt are all in the public domain.
  • The creator chooses to give up their rights, for example with a CC0 license.

Giving credit is still the honest thing to do, and it’s the only way to avoid plagiarism when you present work as part of your own.

Open access research

Scientists traditionally publish in academic journals after peer review, where other experts check their methods and data. Many journals put papers behind a paywall, so only people at schools or companies that pay for subscriptions can read them.

The open access movement aims to make research free for anyone to read and build on. Open access journals, like those from the non-profit Public Library of Science (PLOS), publish every paper under a Creative Commons license.

Easy online publishing has a downside. Some “predatory” journals charge authors to publish but do little or no peer review. In 2009, a researcher submitted a nonsense paper written by a computer program, and a journal accepted it for an $800 fee.

Open data

Scientists also share data in open databases, like DNA sequences, medical images, and climate records such as NOAA’s ocean temperature data. More data lets researchers reach stronger conclusions. But not everyone shares: companies may keep valuable data to themselves, and health data raises serious privacy concerns unless information that identifies people is removed.

Key idea

Public domain works have no copyright restrictions, either because copyright expired or the creator gave it up. Open access makes research free to read and reuse, which helps spread knowledge, but readers still need to judge whether research was carefully peer reviewed. Open data speeds up science but must protect people’s privacy.
