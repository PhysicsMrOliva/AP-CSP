Good enough is good enough

Photos, songs, and videos are huge, and lossless compression barely dents them. Lossy compression shrinks them much more by throwing away the details your eyes and ears are least likely to miss. Today you decide how much to throw away.

1

What is lossy compression?

Last lesson you used lossless compression. Here’s the difference again:

Lossless

Shrinks the file without losing anything. Unpacking it gives back the exact original, bit for bit.

Lossy

Shrinks the file much more by permanently throwing away less important information. You can’t get the exact original back.

Nobody likes losing information. But some files are so large that there just isn’t room to keep everything, and we didn’t need all that data in the first place. That’s often true of photos, videos, and audio recordings: files that try to capture the endless detail of the real world. Remember the random-noise picture in the RLE playground? Real photos and sounds are like that, with almost no exact repeats, so lossless methods can’t shrink them much.

Lossy compression takes a different approach: figure out which details people can’t perceive, and remove those.

Could text be lossy?

Here’s one way to make text smaller by throwing information away: keep the first letter of each word, then delete every other vowel. Type your own text and see how readable it stays.

Compressed

Code.org has its own Lossy Text Compression widget: open it in a new tab after logging in to Code.org, and enter your own text until you understand its algorithm.

Key idea

Lossy compression removes the information people are least likely to notice. It makes files far smaller than lossless compression, but the removed details are gone for good. That’s why text is almost never compressed lossily: losing letters can change the meaning.

Images: keep the brightness, average the color

An uncompressed color image stores 3 bytes per pixel: red, green, and blue. This 480 × 320 picture has 153,600 pixels, so uncompressed it takes 460,800 bytes, about 450 KB.

The human eye is better at noticing differences in brightness than differences in color. Image formats take advantage of that. First they split every pixel into a brightness part and a color part, called chroma. Switch between the views:

Notice how much of the picture lives in the brightness view: edges, shapes, texture. Now zoom in on 8 × 2 pixels of chroma from the edge between two stripes on the balloon. That’s 16 color values. Averaging each 2 × 2 block leaves only 4 values:

Original chroma: 16 values

Each 2 × 2 block averaged: 4 values

Only the block right on the edge changes noticeably, and at normal size you’d never spot it. The averaged version has a quarter of the color information. This is called chroma subsampling, and it’s used in JPEG images and MPEG video. Below, the same averaging is applied to the whole picture, either to the color or to the brightness. Drag the slider and compare.

How JPEG does it

JPEG combines chroma subsampling with a second trick: a math process called the discrete cosine transform (DCT) that finds fine brightness details the eye barely notices so they can be simplified or dropped. Then the results are stored with fewer bits.

When you save a JPEG you pick a quality. In Khan Academy’s example, a 400 × 300 photo of a cat in a hat took 169 KB at 100% quality, 48 KB at 60%, and only 12 KB at 1%, where the green of the eyes started smearing into the fur. This page runs real JPEG compression in your browser, so you can try it yourself. The close-up shows a small patch blown up.

Key idea

JPEG keeps brightness detailed, averages color (chroma subsampling), and uses the DCT to simplify fine brightness detail. The quality setting decides how much is thrown away: lower quality, smaller file, more visible blocks and smudges.

3

Sound: drop what you can’t hear

Your ears have limits too. Audio formats like MP3 analyze a recording and discard sounds outside our hearing, a process called perceptual audio coding. The science of what people can and can’t hear is called psychoacoustics. Use headphones if you can, and start with your volume low.

Limit 1: the highest pitch you can hear

People hear roughly from 20 Hz to 20,000 Hz, and the top end drops with age. Play each tone. (Some laptop speakers can’t play the highest tones at all.)

Limit 2: loud sounds hide quiet ones at the same time

When a loud sound plays, a quieter sound at a similar pitch can become impossible to hear. This is simultaneous masking. Play the quiet beeps by themselves, then add the loud hiss.

Nothing playing.

Limit 3: a sudden sound hides what comes just before and after

A sudden loud sound, like a drum hit, can hide quieter sounds for up to about 200 milliseconds after it, and even about 20 milliseconds before it. This is temporal masking. The striped areas below are when other sounds get hidden.

Try it. A quiet tick plays near a loud drum hit. Change when the tick happens and listen for it.

Cut the highs

Play this melody and lower the cutoff: everything above it is removed. How low can you go before you notice?

Key idea

Perceptual audio coding removes sounds people can’t hear: frequencies above our range, quiet sounds hidden by louder ones at the same time (simultaneous masking), and sounds just before or after a sudden loud one (temporal masking). An MP3 can be about a tenth the size of CD audio.

4

Video

A video is a series of pictures, called frames, often 30 per second. Storing every frame as a full picture would be enormous. But from one frame to the next, most of the picture usually stays the same. So video formats store an occasional full frame, then for the frames in between store mostly what changed.

Below, the picture is split into 144 blocks. Purple blocks changed since the last frame and have to be stored. Try each scene.

Key idea

Video compression stores full frames only now and then, and otherwise stores just what changed. Scenes where everything changes, like fast camera moves or confetti, need far more data, which is why streams sometimes turn blocky during fast action.

5

Quality vs. size

With lossless compression, we can always rebuild 100% of the original. With lossy compression, we lose some percentage of the data: maybe 5%, maybe 10%, maybe 70%. How much to lose depends on the use case.

Khan’s cat photo is a good example. Projected on a big screen for a presentation, it deserves as much detail as possible: lossless PNG, or JPEG at 100%. On a website for people with slow connections, a smaller file loads faster, so a lower quality is the better choice.

Which matters more?

For each situation, decide whether keeping quality or getting a smaller file matters more.

Key idea

Lossy compression is always a trade-off: more compression means a smaller file but lower quality. Choose based on the use case. When every bit matters, use lossless.
